卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.1 性能指标 · Performance Definition: Latency, Throughput, Utilization, Saturation🧠📍 L1 Slides p.4, 5, 9, 10, 40 · L3 Slides p.3, 12, 15

开张第一周，差评里最多的一个字是"慢"。你想马上多招人，可转念一想：客人说的慢，是等得久，还是你一晚上根本接不了几桌？不先说清是哪种慢，就不知道该改哪里。

技术要点

💡 慢是哪一种慢：一句"慢"本身没法改，得拆成四个各管一件事的数——单个等多久、整体做多少、资源多忙、排着多少——再注明在什么负载下、怎么量的，才知道该改哪里

↩ 承接：全书起点

性能（Performance）不是一个数，要用四个数描述，而且每个数都要注明怎么量的：

  • 延迟（Latency）：单个请求花多久
  • 吞吐（Throughput）：单位时间完成多少
  • 利用率（Utilization）：资源有多忙
  • 饱和度（Saturation）：排着没被服务的有多少

⚠️ 每个数都要说清口径（Always qualify the metric）：平均还是最慢那批、读还是写、整机还是每核

卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.2 响应时间与吞吐 · Latency, Response Time and Throughput🧠📍 L2 Slides p.5, 6, 7

你在门口掐表，发现有两个完全不同的数：一桌从点菜到上齐要多久（响应时间），一小时能翻几桌（吞吐）。你以为翻台多就说明快，可翻台最多的那晚，照样有客人等得火冒三丈。

技术要点

💡 每次多久，和每秒多少：一个量单个请求的全程，一个量整体的产量；请求是重叠着走的，所以两者互不决定，不能拿 1 除以"每次多久"当吞吐

↩ 承接：§1.1 把"慢"拆成四个数 → 先把最常被混为一谈的两个（延迟、吞吐）各自定义清楚，并说明它们为什么不能互推（拆开细看）

  • 响应时间（Response Time）：从发出请求到收到完整结果的全部时间，含等待、服务和传结果
  • 吞吐：单位时间完成的工作量，按字节或按次数计；两种换算：字节/秒 = 次/秒 × 每次字节数
  • 符号：次/秒 = 每秒完成的操作数（ops/s），字节/秒 = 每秒搬的数据量（bytes/s）
推导
  1. 一秒里做完若干次（次/秒）
  2. 每次搬同样多的字节（每次字节数；大小不一时取平均）
  3. 一秒搬的总字节 = 次数 × 每次字节数，即 字节/秒 = 次/秒 × 每次字节数

两者互不决定：吞吐很高时，单个请求照样可以很慢。

⚠️ 吞吐 ≠ 1/响应时间：每次 1.3 s 不等于每秒最多 0.77 次，这只在一次只做一个时成立；里面同时有好几个在处理时，时间段互相重叠，这样除会把吞吐估低，不能这样算

⚠️ "延迟"有窄、宽两种口径：先问只算等待和连接，还是全程；对用户承诺的"4 秒内出结果"量的是响应时间

和故事的联系

门口掐表↔测量
一桌从点菜到上齐↔响应时间（Response Time）
一小时翻几桌↔吞吐
翻台最多那晚照样有人等急↔吞吐高时单个请求仍可慢
∵一个量的是个体、一个量的是整体
∴餐馆里"翻台多"和"每桌快"也可以同时不成立
∵同一时刻有好几桌在吃，时间段互相重叠
∴拿 1 除以每桌多久，会把并发系统的吞吐估低

计算样例

题：一块磁盘每秒完成 500 次读，每次读 4 KB，按字节报吞吐是多少？

  1. 公式：字节/秒 = 次/秒 × 每次字节数（两种吞吐口径的换算）
  2. 代入：500 次/s × 4 KB
  3. 结果：2000 KB/s（约 2 MB/s）；"500 次/秒"和"2000 KB/秒"说的是同一份吞吐，报数必须写明是哪种单位

⚠️ 易错：看到每次读要 10 ms，就说这块盘每秒最多 100 次——只在一次只做一个时成立，盘里同时有几个在处理时这样算会估低

卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.3 随机 I/O 与顺序 I/O · Random/Small vs Sequential Disk I/O🧠📍 L2 Slides p.10

后厨那口炒锅（磁盘）一晚上没停过，你以为它出了最多的菜。其实它每次只炒一小份，大半时间花在起锅、刷锅上，忙了一晚，出菜量却远不如别的锅。

技术要点

💡 小块随机读写为什么很忙却搬得少：每次读写 = 固定开销 + 搬字节；块越小，时间越多耗在固定开销上，所以"每秒几次"和"每秒几字节"可以朝相反方向走

↩ 承接：§1.2 说吞吐可以按次数或按字节报 → 同一块盘两种报法会给出相反的印象，这里拆开每次操作的耗时，说明"次数掉了"不等于"变闲了"（补上两种口径之间的缺口）

  • 磁盘（Disk）：持久保存数据的存储设备，断电不丢，比主存慢得多
  • I/O（Input/Output，输入输出）：程序和磁盘、网络等设备之间搬数据

小块随机读写（Small Random I/O）让磁盘把时间耗在每次操作的固定开销上：设备一直在忙，传输的字节数却远低于最大值。（材料外补充）

和故事的联系

炒锅↔磁盘（Disk）
每次只炒一小份↔小块随机 I/O
起锅、刷锅↔每次操作的固定开销
忙了一晚出菜少↔设备一直忙、字节吞吐远低于最大值
∵每次操作都有固定开销，量越小开销占比越大
∴炒小份的锅和做小块 I/O 的盘一样"忙而无产"
卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.4 时间型与容量型利用率 · Utilization: Time-based vs Capacity-based🧠🧮📍 L2 Slides p.5, 9, 10

所以"这口锅很忙"其实有两种意思：一是它这一晚有多少时间在炒，二是它出的菜占了它最大能力的几成。你以为两个说法是一回事，其实锅可以炒了一整晚，出菜却只到上限的两成。

技术要点

💡 忙的时间，和用掉的能力：只看次数说不出忙闲，所以"忙"要么直接量占了多少时间（时间型），要么量离最大吞吐多远（容量型）；小块随机读写下前者已满、后者还远

↩ 承接：§1.3 那块盘同样每秒 100 次，小块只占半秒、大块一秒做不完 → 次数答不了"忙不忙"，于是把 §1.1 的利用率落成两种能直接量的口径（用到新问题）

利用率有两种量法：

  • 时间型利用率（Time-based Utilization）：U = B/T，忙的时间占比
  • 容量型利用率（Capacity-based Utilization）：当前吞吐 ÷ 可持续的最大吞吐
  • 由计数推出的三式：X = C/T（每秒几次），D = B/C（每次占设备多久），X_max = 1/D（这种请求的上限）；例：60 s 里忙 45 s、做完 6000 次 → U = 0.75，X = 100 IOPS，D = 7.5 ms，X_max ≈ 133 IOPS
  • 符号：U = 利用率（0–1），B = 观察期内忙的时间（秒），T = 观察时长（秒），C = 观察期内完成的次数，X = 吞吐（次/秒），D = 每次占设备的时间（秒），IOPS（I/O Operations Per Second，每秒读写次数）

U = B/T 与容量型利用率都是（定义）：只看次数说不出忙闲（同样每秒 100 次，小块只占半秒，大块一秒根本做不完），所以直接量"忙了多少时间"或"离最大吞吐多远"。

推导
  1. T 秒里做完 C 次 → 平均每秒 X = C/T
  2. 忙的 B 秒是这 C 次分着用掉的 → 每次平均占设备 D = B/C
  3. 照这样的请求一直做，一秒的忙时间最多填满一秒，每次占 D 秒 → 一秒最多 1/D 次：X_max = 1/D

⚠️ 时间型 100% ≠ 吞吐到顶：看到"100% 忙"先问哪种口径；报容量型要说清分母（哪种请求下的最大值，按字节还是按次数）

⚠️ 求每次多久，用忙时间 B 去除，不是用观察期 T（那会把闲着的时间也算进去）

和故事的联系

锅这一晚有多少时间在炒↔时间型利用率 U = B/T
出菜占最大能力几成↔容量型利用率
炒了一整晚、出菜只到上限两成↔时间型 100% ≠ 吞吐到顶
∵"占着"和"产出"是两回事
∴一个量设备有没有歇，一个量离上限多远
∵请求小而随机时，设备一直在忙，却只搬了最大吞吐的一部分
∴不能拿"一直忙"推出"已经到顶"

计算样例

题：观察一块磁盘 T = 10 s，其中忙 B = 8 s，完成 C = 400 次读；求 U、X、D、X_max。

  1. 公式：U = B/T，X = C/T，D = B/C，X_max = 1/D（全由计数推出）
  2. 代入：U = 8/10；X = 400/10；D = 8/400；X_max = 1/0.02
  3. 结果：U = 0.8，X = 40 次/s，D = 0.02 s = 20 ms，X_max = 50 次/s；盘忙了八成时间，按次数算也只用了 40/50 = 八成能力

⚠️ 易错：用 T/C = 25 ms 当每次多久，把闲着的 2 s 也算进去，上限就被估成 40 次/s

题2：另一块盘时间型 U = 1（一直忙），每秒做 100 次 10 KB 的小块随机读；这块盘顺序读最多 100 MB/s。按字节算容量型利用率是多少？

  1. 公式：容量型利用率 = 当前吞吐 ÷ 可持续的最大吞吐（分母说清按字节）
  2. 代入：100 × 10 KB/s = 1000 KB/s ≈ 1 MB/s；1 ÷ 100
  3. 结果：约 0.01 = 1%；时间型 100% 忙，字节吞吐却只到上限的 1%，"一直忙"不等于"到顶"
卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.5 利用率上限与瓶颈 · Capacity Ceiling: U ≤ 1🧠🧮📍 L1 Slides p.27–28 · L2 Slides p.9, 11 · L5 Slides p.23, 26 · L10 Slides p.29, 31–32

点单、炒菜、传菜串成一条线。你多招了两个传菜员，翻台数纹丝不动——因为炒锅一小时最多也就忙一小时，它就是瓶颈。钱花在别处，上限一桌也多不出来。

技术要点

💡 一秒最多忙一秒：忙时间 = 次数 × 每次占用，又不能超过总时间，于是吞吐被每次占用时间 D 封顶在 1/D；几台串着时，D 最大的那台说了算

↩ 承接：§1.4 给了 U = B/T 和单台的 X_max = 1/D → 把 B 拆成 X·T·D 代回去得 U = X·D ≤ 1，再从一台推广到一条链的瓶颈（推广）

一个资源一秒最多忙一秒，所以 U = X·D ≤ 1，得到 X ≤ 1/D。几个资源串着时，D 最大的那个（瓶颈 Bottleneck）定出上限 1/D_max。

  • 符号：X = 吞吐（个/秒），D = 每个请求在该资源上占用的总时间（秒），D_max = 各资源中最大的 D，U 见上
推导
  1. 观察 T 秒，每秒做完 X 次 → 共做完 X·T 次
  2. 每次占该资源 D 秒 → 忙时间 B = X·T·D
  3. 代入 U = B/T（§1.4）→ U = X·D
  4. 忙时间 B 是从 T 里切出来的，B ≤ T → U ≤ 1 → X ≤ 1/D
  5. 每个请求要过每一台，各台的约束同时成立，最紧的说了算 → X ≤ min(1/D_i) = 1/D_max（D_i = 第 i 台的 D）

⚠️ D 不是响应时间：D 只算机器干活的时间，不含排队（平台三台 D 合计 0.45 s，响应时间 1.3 s 其余全是排队）

  • 边界：1/D_max 是吞吐的上界，不是预测值；X 等于每秒来的请求数，只在没饱和时成立

和故事的联系

点单、炒菜、传菜串成一条线↔串联的几个资源
炒锅↔瓶颈（Bottleneck），D 最大的那台
传菜员↔非瓶颈资源
多招传菜员翻台不动↔加非瓶颈不提高上限 1/D_max
一小时最多忙一小时↔U = X·D ≤ 1
∵干活的时间不能叠着花
∴串起来的一条线，产量只听最紧的那一环
∵等待可以很多请求叠着等，干活时间却不能叠
∴分母必须是 D：拿 1 除以 D 得天花板，拿 1 除以响应时间就犯了 §1.2 的错

计算样例

题：每个请求依次经 Web 服务器（D = 0.02 s）、应用服务器（D = 0.05 s）、数据库（D = 0.1 s）；求吞吐上限和瓶颈，再求每秒来 8 个时各台利用率。

  1. 公式：X ≤ 1/D_max；没饱和时 X = 每秒来的请求数，U = X·D
  2. 代入：D_max = 0.1 s，1/0.1；U = 8 × 0.02、8 × 0.05、8 × 0.1
  3. 结果：上限 10 个/s，数据库是瓶颈；每秒来 8 个时三台 U = 0.16、0.4、0.8——加 Web 或应用服务器，上限一个也不多

⚠️ 易错：拿响应时间（比如 0.5 s）去除得 2 个/s，把上限估低了 5 倍

题2：同一系统每秒来 12 个，吞吐多少？一分钟后积压多少？

  1. 公式：X ≤ 1/D_max，来得比做得完多的部分只能排队
  2. 代入：X = 10 个/s；积压 = 60 s × (12 − 10) 个/s
  3. 结果：吞吐封顶 10 个/s，一分钟后积压 120 个；多出来的负载不变成吞吐，只变成等待
卷一 · 第1章 把'变慢了'变成可测的数§1.6 拐点与饱和点 · Scalability: Knee Point and Saturation Point; Workload🧠📍 L1 Slides p.27, 28 · L2 Slides p.4, 7

客人越来越多，翻台数一开始跟着涨，你很高兴。到某一晚突然不涨了，门口的队却一下子长出一大截。你以为是服务员偷懒，其实是炒锅快满了，多来的客只能站着排队（拐点）。

技术要点

💡 负载一路加上去会怎样：吞吐先跟着涨，瓶颈快满时拐弯封顶，多来的活不再变成产量、只变成排队；而且不用等到 100% 就开始拐

↩ 承接：§1.5 给出天花板 1/D_max → 把负载从低往高扫一遍，看吞吐和等待在碰到天花板前后怎样变（把一个静态上限展开成一条曲线）

负载（Load，压到系统上的请求量）一路加上去，吞吐先近线性增长；到瓶颈接近满利用率处出现拐点（Knee），吞吐不再涨、等待快速上升；某组件利用率到 100% 是饱和点（Saturation Point）。

⚠️ 吞吐跟着负载涨，只在拐点之前成立；瓶颈饱和后再加负载，只涨排队和响应时间

⚠️ 拐点在饱和点之前或几乎同时：资源"接近"满利用率时等待就已快速上升，不用等到 100%

⚠️ 只盯平均响应时间会晚一步发现走近拐点；请求组成（Request Mix，各类请求各占多少）变了（搜索比例涨），瓶颈的 D 变大，拐点提前

翻台数跟着涨↔拐点前吞吐近线性增长

技术要点

💡 两种 CPU 模式：内核这个"中间人"不靠程序自觉，靠 CPU 硬件分两种模式、让普通程序执行不了特权指令；代价是每次请内核代办都要跨界，花 CPU 周期

卷一 · 第2章 一次请求穿过哪些层§2.4 寄存器与程序计数器 · CPU Registers and Program Counter🧠📍 L6 Slides p.9, 12, 19

厨师递了领料单，要等仓库送料，总管就让他先下灶，灶给别人用。难处来了：料到了，他怎么接着炒？办法是下灶前写张便签"宫保鸡丁，做到第三步"（程序计数器），连手边几样料怎么摆的也记下（寄存器）。你以为得从头再炒，其实看一眼便签就能原样接上。

技术要点

💡 CPU 怎么记住程序执行到哪：程序"停在哪、手里拿着什么"，就是 CPU 里一小组格子的值再加上栈；把它整组存下、整组装回，程序就能被中途打断、再原样接着跑

↩ 承接：§2.3 的 read 进了内核要等磁盘，这期间 CPU 可能去跑别人 → 补课：数据回来时，凭什么能从 read 的下一条指令接着跑（补上 §2.3 留下的缺口）

  • 它原来解决什么问题：把程序的指令一条条放进内存、由 CPU 依次执行的计算机，得有一个格子记着"下一条在哪"，还得有几格极快的地方放正在算的数；后来要让 CPU 能中途被打断去办别的事、办完再回来，就靠把这几个格子整组存下、整组装回。
  • 核心思想：程序"跑到哪了、手里拿着什么"，就是 CPU 里那一小组格子的值；存下它就能停，装回它就能接着跑。
  • 为什么正好用得上：程序调用 read 进了内核（§2.3），数据要等磁盘，这段时间内核把 CPU 交给别的程序（内核管 CPU 调度，§2.1）；难处是数据回来时，它得从 read 的下一条指令接着跑、算到一半的数不能丢——只要那组格子存全了，原样装回就接上，程序自己察觉不到被打断。
  • 程序计数器（Program Counter）：存着下一条指令的地址
  • 寄存器（Register）：CPU 内部存正在用的数据的极快小存储
  • 栈（Stack）：存函数调用和局部变量的内存区

寄存器 + 程序计数器 + 栈，构成一个线程的执行状态。保存它就能暂停线程，装回它就能继续。（材料外补充）

和故事的联系

"做到第三步"↔程序计数器（Program Counter）

便签↔被保存的执行状态
回来接着做↔装回状态、继续执行
∵可以被打断的工作必须能存档
∴只要状态存全了，暂停和继续就不丢进度

💡 内核怎么区分几百个程序：把"家当"和"干活的人"分开——进程拥有内存和打开的文件，线程各带一份执行状态在 CPU 上跑；所以资源按进程算，调度按线程排

↩ 承接：§2.4 说一份执行状态（寄存器 + 程序计数器 + 栈）能整组存下 → 一份执行状态就是一个线程，再把共享的内存和文件圈成进程，§2.1 的内核就能分清几百个程序

卷一 · 第2章 一次请求穿过哪些层§2.6 调度器与上下文切换 · Scheduler, Context Switch and Preemption🧠📍 L6 Slides p.9, 13, 17, 18

技术要点

💡 几颗 CPU 怎么"同时"跑几百个线程：其实不是同时，是轮流——每个线程跑一小段就换人，要等 I/O 的先让位；换人靠的就是把执行状态存下、装上另一份

↩ 承接：§2.5 说调度的单位是线程，§2.4 说执行状态能存能装 → 两者合起来当工具，就能让少量 CPU 轮流跑大量线程（拿它当工具）

调度器（Scheduler）靠分时（Time-sharing，按时间轮流用 CPU）和抢占，让少量 CPU 轮流跑大量线程，过程是：

  1. 可运行的线程进运行队列（Run Queue）排队
  2. 调度器挑一个上 CPU，给一个时间片（Time Slice）
  3. 时间片用完或被更高优先级（Priority，谁先用 CPU 的等级）的线程抢占（Preemption，强行换下正在跑的线程），回队列
  4. 线程要等 I/O 时阻塞（Blocking），让出 CPU；I/O 完成后被唤醒，回队列

换人这个动作叫上下文切换（Context Switch）：存下甲的寄存器，装上乙的。

⚠️ 模式切换 ≠ 上下文切换：模式切换是同一个线程在用户态和内核态之间跨线，每次系统调用都有；上下文切换是 CPU 换了一个线程。立刻办完的系统调用只有前者，要等磁盘的 read 两样都有

⚠️ CPU 很忙 ≠ 请求不在等：等磁盘、等调度器轮到的时间不计入该线程的 CPU 忙碌

技术要点

💡 从缓存（Cache）、内存到磁盘，取一个数要多久：看数据放在哪一层，层与层差好几个数量级；所以一次请求的耗时先看它落了几次慢层，几次落盘就能盖过成千上万次内存访问

↩ 承接：§2.4 的寄存器是存储的塔尖、§1.3 的磁盘在塔底 → 把中间几层补齐，按层给出每次访问要多久（把前面的零件排成一座塔）

  • 缓存：CPU 旁边小而快的存储，放最近用过的数据；L1/L2/L3 是第 1/2/3 级，越往后越大越慢
  • 存储层次（Memory Hierarchy）：数据所在的层次从快到慢是寄存器 → L1/L2/L3 缓存 → 内存 → 磁盘，越往下越大越慢

典型数：

  • 1 个 CPU 周期：约 0.3 ns
  • L1：0.9 ns
  • 内存：100 ns（约 L1 的 111 倍）
  • 固态盘 SSD（Solid State Drive，用闪存芯片存数据）：10–100 µs
  • 机械盘 HDD（Hard Disk Drive，靠转盘和磁头读写）：1–10 ms

总耗时 = 次数 × 每次耗时：读 50 行，全在内存 50 × 100 ns = 5 µs，全在机械盘 50 × 5 ms = 0.25 s，差 5 万倍；20 行在 SSD + 30 行在内存 = 2 ms + 3 µs，先优化 SSD 那 20 次。

  • 符号：n = 访问次数，t = 该层每次访问耗时（秒）
推导
  1. 一次请求里的访问一次接一次地做，每次在该层花 t 秒 → 该层共花 n × t
  2. 各层分别算，再相加 → 总耗时 = Σ n × t（Σ 读作"把各层加起来"）
  • 前提："时间几乎全被磁盘吃掉"只在数据要从磁盘取时成立；数据已在内存里，磁盘那项消失，缓存命中与否成了主角

⚠️ 先比数量级，再数次数：几次慢层访问就能盖过成千上万次快层访问

计算样例

题：一次请求要读 100 次数据，分别全在内存（100 ns/次）、全在 SSD（50 µs/次）、全在机械盘（5 ms/次），各要多久？

  1. 公式：总耗时 = n × t
  2. 代入：100 × 100 ns；100 × 50 µs；100 × 5 ms
  3. 结果：10 µs、5 ms、0.5 s；同样 100 次，落在哪一层让时间差了 5 万倍

题2：1000 次内存访问 + 2 次机械盘访问，总共多久？哪部分是大头？

  1. 公式：总耗时 = Σ n × t（各层分别算再相加）
  2. 代入：1000 × 100 ns = 100 µs；2 × 5 ms = 10 ms
  3. 结果：约 10.1 ms，2 次落盘占 99%；先比数量级，优化那 2 次盘访问才有用

⚠️ 易错：单位混着加（100 + 10 = 110），要先统一成 µs 或 ms

⚠️ 缓存温度链：切换太多 → 线程在 CPU 上待不久 → 缓存变冷（Cold Cache，缓存里还没有要用的数据，来不及变热）→ LLC（Last-Level Cache，末级缓存，CPU 里离内存最近的那级）命中率（Hit Ratio，要的数据恰好在缓存里的比例）低 → 停顿周期（Stall Cycles，CPU 原地等数据、没推进指令的时钟周期）多 → IPC（Instructions Per Cycle，每个时钟周期完成几条指令）低 → CPU 看着很忙

  • 案例数（VM 即 Virtual Machine 虚拟机 vs 容器 Container，同一服务的两种部署方式）：VM 每秒约 200 万次上下文切换；LLC 命中率约 28–31% 对 87–93%；IPC 0.56–0.59 对 1.47–1.61；VM 的 LLC 也更小（33 MB 对 45 MB）
  • 末级缓存命中率 = 1 − LLC 未命中数 ÷ LLC 引用数；pmcarch 的 LLC% 列就是命中率，不是未命中率
  • 符号：LLC 引用数 = 访问末级缓存的次数（次），LLC 未命中数 = 其中未命中（Cache Miss，这一级没有、只好去下一级取）、要去内存取的次数（次）
推导
  1. 每次引用末级缓存，要么命中、要么未命中 → 引用数 = 命中数 + 未命中数
  2. 命中率按定义是 命中数 ÷ 引用数
  3. 把 命中数 = 引用数 − 未命中数 代进去 → 命中率 = 1 − 未命中数 ÷ 引用数

⚠️ 诱人错答"切换本身吃掉 CPU"：%sys（内核态时间占 CPU 的百分比，对应 %usr 是用户态）只有 8.5%，撑不起 98%；大头是缓存冷导致的用户态停顿周期

和故事的联系

料刚摆顺就被换下灶↔线程在 CPU 上待不久
换上来的人手边空空↔缓存变冷（Cold Cache）、LLC 命中率低
每样都得重新去冰箱拿↔缓存未命中、去内存取
灶占着、菜出得慢↔停顿周期多、IPC 低、CPU 看着很忙
∵停顿周期照样记作 CPU 忙
∴换人太勤的灶看着满负荷，其实大半拍在原地等料
∵每次换线程都丢掉手边的热数据
∴切换多到线程只能待几微秒时，缓存永远热不起来，代价远大于切换动作本身

题：pmcarch 在一台服务器上报 LLC 引用数 1000 万次、LLC 未命中数 700 万次，末级缓存命中率是多少？

  1. 公式：末级缓存命中率 = 1 − LLC 未命中数 ÷ LLC 引用数
  2. 代入：1 − 700 万 ÷ 1000 万 = 1 − 0.7
  3. 结果：0.3 = 30%；七成访问要去内存取（每次约 100 ns），和 VM 案例的冷缓存一个水平，IPC 多半很低

卷一 · 第3章 研究一个系统有哪几种办法§3.6 解析模型 · Analytic Model: Inputs and Outputs🧠📍 L3 Slides p.3–5, 12

坐下来算账之前，得先备齐原料：每小时来几位、各点什么、一道菜要过几道工序、有几口锅、火多旺（模型输入）。你以为缺一两样也能估，其实缺哪样都得写明是估的，不然算出来的数没人敢信。

技术要点

💡 解析模型吃什么、吐什么：它是一台"参数进、性能出"的机器，吃负载和软硬件两类参数、吐响应时间和吞吐；它不会凭空造信息，输出只和最没把握的那个输入一样可信

↩ 承接：§3.1 说解析模型能在系统建成前评估 → 把它拆开看输入输出，并拿 §1.5 的 U = X·D、X ≤ 1/D 当最小的模型实算一遍（拆开细看）

解析模型（Analytic Model）是系统的数学表示：吃进输入参数，吐出响应时间、吞吐等性能度量。

  • 模型输入（Model Inputs）：分两类，每个重要输入都要有测量、估计或写明的假设
  • 负载参数（Workload Parameters）：资源需求，描述外面压过来多少活（来多少、各是什么请求），如到达率（Arrival Rate，每秒新来几个请求）λ、请求组成（Request Mix，各类请求各占多少）
  • 服务时间（Service Time）：一个请求被服务要多久，属于软硬件参数
  • 软硬件参数（System Parameters）：资源容量，描述系统有多少设备、各多快

⚠️ 请求组成填错，全盘错：平台若把 12 个请求全算进库，12 × 0.25 = 3，一秒忙三秒，不可能；只有 25% 进库，λ = 3/s，U = 0.75

算例四步：

  1. λ = 总到达率 × 进库比例 = 12 × 0.25 = 3/s
  2. U = λ·D = 3 × 0.25 = 0.75
  3. X ≤ 1/D = 4 > 3，不饱和，X = 3/s
  4. 加到两台：每台 U = (λ/2)·D = 0.375
  • 符号：λ = 进库的到达率（个/秒），U = 数据库利用率（0–1），D = 每个请求在库里要的服务时间（0.25 秒），X = 吞吐（个/秒）
推导
  1. 全站每秒来的请求里，只有进库那一份落到库上 → λ = 总到达率 × 进库比例
  2. 每个进库请求占库 D 秒 → 库每秒忙 λ·D 秒
  3. 代入 U = B/T（T 取 1 秒，§1.4）→ U = λ·D
  4. 由 §1.5，库的吞吐 X ≤ 1/D；λ < 1/D 时来得及做，X = λ

和故事的联系

备齐原料↔模型输入（Model Inputs）
每小时来几位、各点什么↔负载参数（Workload Parameters）
几道工序、几口锅、火多旺↔软硬件参数（System Parameters）
算出来的数↔解析模型输出的性能度量
缺哪样写明是估的↔未测的输入要写成假设
∵模型就是"输入参数 → 输出性能"
∴原料齐不齐决定菜能不能做
∵模型不会凭空造出信息
∴输出的可信度不超过输入，填不进的格必须写成假设

计算样例

题：Web 服务每秒收 20 个请求，其中 40% 要查数据库，每个查询占库 D = 0.1 s；求进库的 λ、库的 U，是否饱和？

  1. 公式：λ = 总到达率 × 进库比例；U = λ·D；X ≤ 1/D
  2. 代入：λ = 20 × 0.4 = 8/s；U = 8 × 0.1；1/D = 10/s
  3. 结果：λ = 8 个/s，U = 0.8，8 < 10 不饱和，X = 8 个/s；库忙八成，还有余量

题2：请求组成变了，进库比例涨到 60%，再算一次。

You stand at the door with a stopwatch and find two completely different numbers: how long a table takes from ordering until every dish has arrived (response time), and how many tables turn over in an hour (throughput). You assumed more turnover meant faster service, yet on the night with the most turnover, some customers were still fuming about the wait.

Technical points

💡 How long each one takes vs. how many per second: one measures a single request from start to finish, the other measures total output. Requests overlap in time, so neither one determines the other — you can’t take 1 divided by “how long each one takes” as the throughput.

↩ Builds on: §1.1 broke “slow” into four numbers → here the two most often confused (latency and throughput) each get a clear definition, along with why you can’t derive one from the other (a closer look)

  • Response time: the whole time from sending a request to receiving the complete result, including waiting, service, and sending the result back
  • Throughput: work completed per unit of time, counted in bytes or in operations; to convert between the two: bytes/s = ops/s × bytes per op
  • Symbols: ops/s = operations completed per second, bytes/s = amount of data moved per second
Derivation
  1. Some number of operations finish in one second (ops/s)
  2. Each one moves the same number of bytes (bytes per op; if sizes vary, use the average)
  3. Total bytes moved in one second = number of ops × bytes per op, i.e. bytes/s = ops/s × bytes per op

Neither determines the other: even when throughput is high, an individual request can still be slow.

⚠️ Throughput ≠ 1/response time: 1.3 s per request does not mean at most 0.77 per second. That only holds when requests are handled one at a time; when several are in progress at once, their time spans overlap, and dividing like this underestimates throughput. Don’t calculate it this way.

⚠️ “Latency” has a narrow and a broad meaning: first ask whether it counts only waiting and connection time, or the whole trip. A promise to users of “results within 4 seconds” is measured as response time.

∵Several tables are eating at the same moment, so their time spans overlap

Worked example

Problem: a disk completes 500 reads per second, 4 KB each. What is its throughput in bytes?

  1. Formula: bytes/s = ops/s × bytes per op (converting between the two throughput units)
  2. Substitute: 500 ops/s × 4 KB
  3. Result: 2000 KB/s (about 2 MB/s). “500 ops/s” and “2000 KB/s” describe the same throughput, so a reported number must say which unit it uses

⚠️ Common mistake: seeing that each read takes 10 ms and concluding the disk can do at most 100 per second. That only holds when reads are handled one at a time; when the disk has several in progress at once, this underestimates it.

So “this wok is busy” actually has two meanings: how much of the evening it spent cooking, and what fraction of its maximum output it actually produced. You assumed they were the same thing, but a wok can cook all night and still put out only a fifth of what it could.

Technical points

💡 Time spent busy vs. capacity used: counting operations alone can’t tell you how busy something is, so “busy” means either measuring directly how much of the time is taken up (time-based) or measuring how far you are from maximum throughput (capacity-based). With small random reads and writes, the first is already full while the second is still far off.

↩ Builds on: in §1.3 the disk runs the same 100 ops per second, but small blocks fill only half a second while large blocks can’t finish within one → op counts can’t answer “is it busy?”, so §1.1’s utilization becomes two directly measurable definitions (applied to a new question)

Utilization can be measured two ways:

  • Time-based utilization: U = B/T, the fraction of time spent busy
  • Capacity-based utilization: current throughput ÷ maximum sustainable throughput
  • Three formulas from counts: X = C/T (ops per second), D = B/C (how long each op occupies the device), X_max = 1/D (the ceiling for this kind of request); e.g. busy 45 s out of 60 s with 6000 ops done → U = 0.75, X = 100 IOPS, D = 7.5 ms, X_max ≈ 133 IOPS
  • Symbols: U = utilization (0–1), B = busy time during the observation period (seconds), T = length of the observation period (seconds), C = number of ops completed during the observation period, X = throughput (ops/s), D = time each op occupies the device (seconds), IOPS (I/O Operations Per Second)

U = B/T and capacity-based utilization are both definitions: counting operations alone can’t tell you how busy something is (at the same 100 ops per second, small blocks take only half a second, while large blocks can’t even finish in one), so you measure directly “how much time was spent busy” or “how far from maximum throughput”.

Derivation
  1. C ops are done in T seconds → on average X = C/T per second
  2. The B busy seconds are shared out among those C ops → each op occupies the device for D = B/C on average
  3. Keep issuing requests like these: busy time can fill at most one second per second, and each op takes D seconds → at most 1/D ops per second: X_max = 1/D

⚠️ 100% time-based ≠ throughput maxed out: when you see “100% busy”, first ask which definition is meant; when reporting capacity-based utilization, state the denominator (the maximum for which kind of request, in bytes or in ops)

⚠️ To find the time per op, divide the busy time B, not the observation period T (that would count idle time too)

Worked example

Problem: a disk is observed for T = 10 s; it is busy for B = 8 s and completes C = 400 reads. Find U, X, D, X_max.

  1. Formula: U = B/T, X = C/T, D = B/C, X_max = 1/D (all derived from counts)
  2. Substitute: U = 8/10; X = 400/10; D = 8/400; X_max = 1/0.02
  3. Result: U = 0.8, X = 40 ops/s, D = 0.02 s = 20 ms, X_max = 50 ops/s. The disk was busy 80% of the time, and by op count it also used only 40/50 = 80% of its capacity

⚠️ Common mistake: using T/C = 25 ms as the time per op also counts the 2 idle seconds, so the ceiling gets estimated at 40 ops/s

Problem 2: another disk has time-based U = 1 (always busy) and does 100 small random 10 KB reads per second; its maximum sequential read rate is 100 MB/s. What is its capacity-based utilization in bytes?

  1. Formula: capacity-based utilization = current throughput ÷ maximum sustainable throughput (state that the denominator is in bytes)
  2. Substitute: 100 × 10 KB/s = 1000 KB/s ≈ 1 MB/s; 1 ÷ 100
  3. Result: about 0.01 = 1%. It is 100% busy by time, yet its byte throughput reaches only 1% of the ceiling — “always busy” doesn’t mean “maxed out”

Technical points

💡 A second can hold at most one second of work: busy time = number of ops × time each op occupies, and it can’t exceed the total time, so throughput is capped at 1/D by the per-op occupancy D. When several resources are in series, the one with the largest D decides.

↩ Builds on: §1.4 gave U = B/T and a single device’s X_max = 1/D → split B into X·T·D and substitute back to get U = X·D ≤ 1, then generalize from one device to the bottleneck of a chain (generalization)

A resource can be busy at most one second per second, so U = X·D ≤ 1, which gives X ≤ 1/D. When several resources are in series, the one with the largest D (the bottleneck) sets the ceiling at 1/D_max.

  • Symbols: X = throughput (requests/s), D = total time each request occupies that resource (seconds), D_max = the largest D among the resources, U see above
Derivation
  1. Observe for T seconds with X completed per second → X·T completed in total
  2. Each occupies the resource for D seconds → busy time B = X·T·D
  3. Substitute into U = B/T (§1.4) → U = X·D
  4. Busy time B is carved out of T, so B ≤ T → U ≤ 1 → X ≤ 1/D
  5. Every request passes through every device, so all the constraints hold at once and the tightest one wins → X ≤ min(1/D_i) = 1/D_max (D_i = the D of device i)

⚠️ D is not response time: D counts only the time the machine is doing work, not queueing (on the platform, the three devices’ D values add up to 0.45 s; the rest of the 1.3 s response time is all queueing)

  • Limits: 1/D_max is an upper bound on throughput, not a prediction; X equals the number of requests arriving per second only when the system isn’t saturated

Technical points

↩ Builds on: §2.1 said the kernel manages all resources → the follow-up question is "what actually stops a program from touching the hardware itself?": the hardware enforces it by mode, which also brings in the cost of every crossing (filling in the mechanism behind §2.1)

  • Kernel mode: the CPU has full access to devices and can execute privileged instructions (instructions only the kernel may run, such as operating devices directly)
  • User mode: the mode ordinary programs run in; for privileged operations they have to ask the kernel

Crossing between the two modes is called a mode switch, and each one costs CPU cycles (one tick of the clock).

⚠️ Privilege isn't something programs "choose" to respect; the hardware refuses to execute based on the current mode

A cook hands in an ingredient request and has to wait for the storeroom to deliver, so the manager has him step away from the stove and gives it to someone else. Here's the catch: when the ingredients arrive, how does he pick up where he left off? The trick is to jot a note before stepping away, "Kung Pao chicken, up to step three" (the program counter), and also note how the ingredients at hand were laid out (the registers). You'd think he'd have to start the dish over, but one glance at the note and he carries on exactly where he was.

Technical points

💡 How the CPU remembers where a program is: where a program "stopped and what it's holding" is just the values in a small set of slots inside the CPU, plus the stack. Save the whole set and load it back, and the program can be interrupted midway and resume exactly as it was

↩ Builds on: in §2.3, read enters the kernel and waits for the disk, and meanwhile the CPU may run someone else → the missing piece: when the data comes back, how can the program carry on from the instruction right after read (closing the gap §2.3 left open)

  • The problem it originally solved: a computer that keeps a program's instructions in memory and has the CPU execute them one by one needs a slot that remembers "where the next one is," plus a few extremely fast slots for the numbers being worked on. Later, to let the CPU be interrupted midway, go do something else, and then come back, the answer was to save these slots as a set and load them back as a set.
  • Core idea: "how far a program has got and what it's holding" is simply the values in that small set of CPU slots. Save them and it can stop; load them back and it carries on.
  • Why it fits here: the program calls read and enters the kernel (§2.3). The data has to wait for the disk, and in the meantime the kernel hands the CPU to another program (the kernel handles CPU scheduling, §2.1). The hard part is that when the data comes back, the program must continue from the instruction after read without losing any half-finished values. As long as that set of slots was saved in full, loading it back picks things up seamlessly, and the program never notices it was interrupted.
  • Program counter: holds the address of the next instruction
  • Register: a tiny, extremely fast store inside the CPU for the data currently in use
  • Stack: the memory region that holds function calls and local variables

Registers + program counter + stack make up a thread's execution state. Save it and the thread can pause; load it back and it continues. (Supplementary, beyond the course materials)

∵Work that can be interrupted has to be able to save its progress

Vol. 1 · Ch. 2 What Layers a Request Passes Through§2.6 The Scheduler and Context Switches · Scheduler, Context Switch and Preemption🧠📍 L6 Slides p.9, 13, 17, 18

There are only a few burners (CPUs), but dozens of cooks. The head chef runs the rota (the scheduler): each cook stir-fries for a short stretch, then hands over to the next; anyone waiting on a delivery from the storeroom steps away from the stove and lets someone else have it. You might think it's simpler to let one cook finish before switching, but then the burner sits idle while they wait for ingredients.

Technical points

💡 How a few CPUs run hundreds of threads "at the same time": they don't, they take turns. Each thread runs for a short stretch and then hands over, and anything waiting on I/O steps aside first. The handover works by saving one thread's execution state and loading another's

↩ Builds on: §2.5 says the unit of scheduling is the thread, and §2.4 says execution state can be saved and restored → put the two together and a few CPUs can take turns running a great many threads (using it as a tool)

The scheduler uses time-sharing (taking turns on the CPU over time) and preemption to let a few CPUs take turns running many threads. It works like this:

  1. Runnable threads line up in the run queue
  2. The scheduler picks one, puts it on a CPU, and gives it a time slice
  3. When the time slice runs out, or a thread with higher priority (the rank that decides who gets the CPU first) preempts it (forcibly swaps out the running thread), it goes back to the queue
  4. When a thread has to wait on I/O, it blocks and gives up the CPU; once the I/O completes it is woken up and goes back to the queue

The act of swapping is called a context switch: save A's registers, load B's.

⚠️ Mode switch ≠ context switch: a mode switch is the same thread crossing between user mode and kernel mode, which happens on every system call; a context switch is the CPU changing to a different thread. A system call that completes immediately involves only the former; a read that has to wait on disk involves both

⚠️ A busy CPU ≠ requests aren't waiting: time spent waiting on disk, or waiting for the scheduler to get round to a thread, doesn't count toward that thread's CPU busy time

∴Everyone has to queue, a third party decides by fixed rules who goes first, and anyone waiting on something else steps aside instead of hogging the resource while idle

Vol. 1 · Ch. 2 What Layers a Request Passes Through§2.7 CPU Caches and the Memory Hierarchy · CPU Caches and the Memory Hierarchy · Time Scales of System Latencies (1/2)🧠🧮📍 L2 Slides p.8 · L6 Slides p.22 · L8 Slides p.7 · L12 Slides p.15, 18

The ingredients for a dish might be right at hand (cache), in the fridge (memory), or in a warehouse outside town (disk). Reaching for something takes a second, the fridge a few minutes, the warehouse a whole day: the distances differ by several orders of magnitude.

Technical points

💡 How long it takes to fetch a value from cache, memory, or disk depends on which layer the data lives in, and the layers differ by orders of magnitude. So when sizing up a request's time, first count how many times it hits a slow layer: a few trips to disk can outweigh thousands of memory accesses

↩ Builds on: the registers from §2.4 sit at the top of the storage pyramid and the disk from §1.3 at the bottom → fill in the layers between and give each one a cost per access (arranging the earlier parts into a pyramid)

  • Cache: small, fast storage next to the CPU that holds recently used data; L1/L2/L3 are levels 1/2/3, each larger and slower than the one before
  • Memory hierarchy: from fastest to slowest, data can live in registers → L1/L2/L3 caches → memory → disk; the further down, the larger and slower

Typical numbers:

  • 1 CPU cycle: about 0.3 ns
  • L1: 0.9 ns
  • Memory: 100 ns (about 111× L1)
  • SSD (solid-state drive, stores data on flash memory chips): 10–100 µs
  • HDD (hard disk drive, reads and writes with spinning platters and a moving head): 1–10 ms

Total time = number of accesses × time per access. Reading 50 rows takes 50 × 100 ns = 5 µs if they're all in memory, and 50 × 5 ms = 0.25 s if they're all on a hard disk: a 50,000× difference. With 20 rows on SSD + 30 in memory, it's 2 ms + 3 µs, so optimize those 20 SSD reads first.

  • Symbols: n = number of accesses, t = time per access at that layer (seconds)
Derivation
  1. The accesses in a request happen one after another, each taking t seconds at that layer → that layer costs n × t in total
  2. Work out each layer separately, then add them up → total time = Σ n × t (Σ means "add up across the layers")
  • Assumption: "disk eats almost all the time" only holds when the data has to come from disk; once it's already in memory, the disk term disappears and cache hits vs misses take center stage

⚠️ Compare orders of magnitude first, then count: a few slow-layer accesses can outweigh thousands of fast-layer ones

How it maps to the story

Right at hand↔Cache
The fridge↔Memory
The warehouse outside town↔Disk
A second to reach, minutes for the fridge, a day for the warehouse↔Access times differ by orders of magnitude between layers
∵How long it takes to fetch something depends on which layer it sits in
∴The ratios between distances map straight onto the time steps of the memory hierarchy
∵A hard disk is slower than memory by 250 ms ÷ 5 µs = 50,000×
∴Once a request touches disk, you can set the bit of CPU computation aside for now; only when everything is in memory does the difference come down to whether the cache is hot or cold

Worked example

Q: A request reads data 100 times. How long does it take if the data is all in memory (100 ns each), all on SSD (50 µs each), or all on a hard disk (5 ms each)?

  1. Formula: total time = n × t
  2. Plug in: 100 × 100 ns; 100 × 50 µs; 100 × 5 ms
  3. Result: 10 µs, 5 ms, 0.5 s. The same 100 accesses differ 50,000× in time depending on which layer they land in

Q2: 1000 memory accesses + 2 hard-disk accesses. How long in total, and which part dominates?

  1. Formula: total time = Σ n × t (work out each layer, then add)
  2. Plug in: 1000 × 100 ns = 100 µs; 2 × 5 ms = 10 ms
  3. Result: about 10.1 ms, with the 2 disk accesses taking 99%. Compare orders of magnitude first: only optimizing those 2 disk accesses will make a difference

⚠️ Common mistake: adding mixed units (100 + 10 = 110). Convert everything to µs or ms first

But having everything at hand doesn't guarantee speed. A cook has only just got their ingredients laid out when the head chef swaps them off the stove; the new cook starts empty-handed and has to go back to the fridge for everything (the cache goes cold). The stove stays occupied and looks busy, yet the dishes come out slowly.

Technical points

💡 Why things can be slow even when all the data is in memory: a cache only warms up if a thread stays on the CPU long enough. Switch threads too often and the cache stays cold; the CPU spends most of its cycles stalled, waiting for data, yet it still gets recorded as "busy"

↩ Builds on: takes the memory hierarchy from (1/2) and adds the context switches from §2.6. Swap too often and the gaps between layers turn into invisible stalls

⚠️ The cache-temperature chain: too many switches → threads don't stay on the CPU for long → cold cache (the cache doesn't yet hold the data that's needed and never gets time to warm up) → low LLC (last-level cache, the level inside the CPU closest to memory) hit ratio (the fraction of requested data that happens to be in the cache) → more stall cycles (clock cycles where the CPU sits waiting for data without advancing any instructions) → low IPC (instructions per cycle, how many instructions complete each clock cycle) → the CPU looks busy

  • Case numbers (VM, i.e. virtual machine, vs container, two ways to deploy the same service): the VM did about 2 million context switches per second; LLC hit ratio about 28–31% vs 87–93%; IPC 0.56–0.59 vs 1.47–1.61; the VM's LLC was also smaller (33 MB vs 45 MB)
  • LLC hit ratio = 1 − LLC misses ÷ LLC references; pmcarch's LLC% column is the hit ratio, not the miss ratio
  • Symbols: LLC references = number of accesses to the last-level cache (count); LLC misses = how many of those missed (a cache miss: the data isn't at this level, so it has to be fetched from the next one down) and had to go to memory (count)
Derivation
  1. Every reference to the last-level cache either hits or misses → references = hits + misses
  2. By definition, hit ratio = hits ÷ references
  3. Substitute hits = references − misses → hit ratio = 1 − misses ÷ references

⚠️ Tempting wrong answer: "the switching itself eats the CPU". %sys (the share of CPU time spent in kernel mode; %usr is its user-mode counterpart) is only 8.5%, nowhere near enough to explain 98%; the bulk is user-mode stall cycles caused by cold caches

How it maps to the story

Swapped off the stove right after laying out the ingredients↔Threads don't stay on the CPU for long

∵Stall cycles still count as the CPU being busy
∴A stove that swaps cooks too often looks fully loaded, but spends most of its time waiting for ingredients
∵Every thread switch throws away the hot data at hand
∴When switches are so frequent that a thread only gets a few microseconds, the cache never warms up, and that costs far more than the switch itself

Worked example

Q: pmcarch on a server reports 10 million LLC references and 7 million LLC misses. What is the last-level cache hit ratio?

  1. Formula: LLC hit ratio = 1 − LLC misses ÷ LLC references
  2. Plug in: 1 − 7 million ÷ 10 million = 1 − 0.7
  3. Result: 0.3 = 30%. Seven in ten accesses go to memory (about 100 ns each), the same cold-cache territory as the VM case, so IPC is probably low

⚠️ Common mistake: reading pmcarch's LLC% column as the miss ratio and concluding "only 30% missed, so the cache is doing fine"

Vol. 1 · Ch. 3 Ways to Study a System§3.6 Analytic Models · Analytic Model: Inputs and Outputs🧠📍 L3 Slides p.3–5, 12

Before you sit down to work out the numbers, you need all your ingredients ready: how many guests come per hour, what each one orders, how many steps a dish goes through, how many pots you have, how hot the fire burns (the model inputs). You'd think you could still estimate with one or two missing, but whatever's missing has to be marked as a guess, or nobody will trust the numbers you come up with.

Technical points

💡 What an analytic model takes in and gives back: it's a "parameters in, performance out" machine. It takes two kinds of parameters, workload and system, and gives back response time and throughput. It can't conjure information out of thin air: the output is only as trustworthy as its shakiest input

↩ Builds on: §3.1 says analytic models can evaluate a system before it's built → here we open one up to look at its inputs and outputs, and work through §1.5's U = X·D, X ≤ 1/D as the smallest possible model (a closer look)

An analytic model is a mathematical representation of a system: it takes input parameters and produces performance measures such as response time and throughput.

  • Model inputs: come in two kinds; every important input needs a measurement, an estimate, or an explicitly stated assumption
  • Workload parameters: resource demands, describing how much work is coming in from outside (how many requests, and of what kinds), e.g. arrival rate (new requests per second) λ and request mix (the share of each request type)
  • Service time: how long it takes to serve one request; counts as a system parameter
  • System parameters: resource capacity, describing how many devices the system has and how fast each one is

⚠️ Get the request mix wrong and everything is wrong: if the platform counted all 12 requests as hitting the database, 12 × 0.25 = 3, i.e. busy for three seconds every second, which is impossible; only 25% go to the database, so λ = 3/s, U = 0.75

The example in four steps:

  1. λ = total arrival rate × fraction going to the DB = 12 × 0.25 = 3/s
  2. U = λ·D = 3 × 0.25 = 0.75
  3. X ≤ 1/D = 4 > 3, not saturated, so X = 3/s
  4. Scale out to two servers: each has U = (λ/2)·D = 0.375
  • Symbols: λ = arrival rate into the database (requests/s), U = database utilization (0–1), D = service time each request needs at the database (0.25 s), X = throughput (requests/s)
Derivation
  1. Of all the requests reaching the site each second, only the share that goes to the database lands on it → λ = total arrival rate × fraction going to the DB
  2. Each DB request occupies the database for D seconds → the database is busy for λ·D seconds every second
  3. Plug into U = B/T (with T = 1 second, §1.4) → U = λ·D
  4. By §1.5, the database's throughput X ≤ 1/D; when λ < 1/D it keeps up, so X = λ

Worked example

Problem: A web service receives 20 requests per second; 40% of them query the database, and each query occupies the database for D = 0.1 s. Find λ into the database and the database's U. Is it saturated?

  1. Formula: λ = total arrival rate × fraction going to the DB; U = λ·D; X ≤ 1/D
  2. Plug in: λ = 20 × 0.4 = 8/s; U = 8 × 0.1; 1/D = 10/s
  3. Result: λ = 8 requests/s, U = 0.8; 8 < 10, so it's not saturated and X = 8 requests/s. The database is 80% busy, with some headroom left

Problem 2: The request mix changes and the fraction going to the DB rises to 60%. Work it out again.

  1. Formula: same as above
  2. Plug in: λ = 20 × 0.6 = 12/s; U = 12 × 0.1 = 1.2
  3. Result: U = 1.2 means being busy for 1.2 seconds every second, which can't happen: the database is saturated, throughput caps at 10 requests/s, and 2 requests pile up every second. Total traffic didn't change at all; just the mix shifted, and the database is overloaded

⚠️ Common mistake: reporting U > 1 as the answer without realizing it means "the input is impossible / the system is saturated"

Vol. 1 · Ch. 3 Ways to Study a System§3.7 Queueing Theory and Operational Analysis · Queueing Theory vs Operational Analysis🧠📍 L3 Slides p.11, 13

There are two ways to do the math. One is to pull out last month's ledger (how many guests came in, how many bills were settled, how many hours the cashier was on duty) and simply divide. The other works like a weather forecast: assume guests fall at random like rain, then work out all the possibilities.

Technical points

💡 Do you write unpredictable arrivals and service times into the math, or just look at averages? Operational analysis uses only the totals you counted and gets you averages and bounds; queueing theory pays for a distribution assumption and gets you the whole distribution

↩ Builds on: §3.6's example used only one average, "each search occupies the database for 0.25 s on average", and got stuck on "when the next request arrives and how long this one takes are unpredictable" → this is where the two modeling routes split off (fills the gap in §3.6)

Two modeling routes:

  • Operational analysis: uses only quantities counted over a period of time, yielding averages plus upper and lower bounds
  • Queueing theory: treats arrivals and service as random variables, and relies on distribution assumptions to compute the whole distribution

Models and measurements each lose different things, so use each one to check the other.

  • Prerequisite: queueing theory pays with a distribution assumption (which probability distributions arrivals and service times follow) and gets the distribution itself in return (e.g. "what fraction wait longer than 4 seconds"); operational analysis needs no distribution assumption, and pays instead with a stretch of real observed data
